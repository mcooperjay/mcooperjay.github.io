Everyone has a camera

So why do people still pay photographers?

The camera was never the skill. Knowing what’s worth shooting, and whether the image is real, is.

Analytics is the same. Everyone has the camera now: dashboards, models, AI.

Soften the “15 years ago” claim from the old draft; “once needed professional gear” is safe and doesn’t invite a debate about camera specs. Everyone in the room loves analytics, so the point isn’t “you’re wrong,” it’s “the camera isn’t the skill.” Deliver it as an observation, not a challenge.

Noise

A striker is 9-for-10 on penalties

It’s a shootout Saturday. Is he your designated taker?

Raw math says 90%. Your gut says “…it’s ten kicks.”

Ten kicks is a grainy photo. You can’t tell a face from a smudge.

What helps is what you already know about what you’re looking at: penalties go in about three in four times. That’s a prior.

Setup only. The tension: the raw number is the only thing on screen, but you carry two pieces of information, the league rate and his ten kicks. Bayesian statistics is the rule for combining them.

Prior + evidence = posterior

\[ \theta \sim \text{Beta}(31,\,9) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta \mid \text{9 makes, 1 miss} \sim \text{Beta}(40,\,10) \]

The update is addition: 31 + 9 makes, 9 + 1 miss. And the answer is a distribution, so you can ask: how likely is he actually better than average?

Prior Beta(31, 9), mean 77.5%, worth about 40 kicks. Posterior Beta(40, 10), mean 80%. A decision-maker doesn’t need “80%.” They need “two in three odds he’s better than average, and here’s how much to trust that.” A point estimate can’t give you that.

The prior is an assumption, said out loud

With ten kicks, your assumption matters. With a hundred, the data takes over. More exposures sharpen the picture.

Every model has a prior. A model that doesn’t show you one is still using it.

Weak prior is worth about 4 kicks, moderate about 40, strong about 100. The usual objection to Bayes is “isn’t the prior just your opinion?” Answer: yes, and it is stated, adjustable, and overwhelmed by evidence. This sets up the NBA project: thousands of players is the right-hand panel.

My own project: does pedigree predict a longer NBA career?

With Jonas Schiel. “Blue blood” programs: Duke, North Carolina, UCLA, Indiana, Kentucky, Kansas.

Career length is a count, right-skewed. Model: Poisson likelihood, Gamma prior. Prior expectation: about 5 years for blue-blood alumni, about 3.5 for everyone else.

Same logic as the penalties. Different conjugate pair, much more data.

Numbers from your write-up: 95% CI for mean career length (5.09, 5.51) vs (4.27, 4.40); difference (0.74, 1.19). Likely questions: (1) “Didn’t you pick different priors for each group?” Yes (5 vs 3.5 years). With this much data the intervals are driven by the data, but the cleanest answer is a sensitivity check: refit with the SAME prior for both groups and report that the conclusion holds. Do this before you present if you can; it turns a vulnerability into the strongest slide. (2) “Is Poisson right?” Poisson forces variance = mean and allows zero years; real careers are overdispersed and at least one year. Say you treated it as a known simplification (your write-up already does) and that a negative binomial or zero-truncated model would be the next step.

What the model can and can’t say

Career longer than Blue-blood Everyone else
5 years 42.8% 27.1%
10 years 1.8% 0.76%

There’s a real gap: the 95% credible interval for the difference is 0.74 to 1.19 years.

What I can’t say: that the school causes it. Top prospects pick top programs. And the model treats each season as independent, which careers aren’t.

Knowing what your model can’t say is part of the model.

This is the “Noise” test applied to your own work: a real, quantified difference, reported with its limits. Next step you named in the write-up: control for draft position, era, and position; or update the blue-blood definition (UCLA). If asked “so does it matter?”: it predicts, it doesn’t prove cause.

Backup

What predicts an NHL player’s plus-minus?

With Claire Peterson and Anzlei Larsen. Multiple linear regression, 1,000-iteration cross-validation, NHL seasons 2000-01 to 2024-25.

Adjusted R² = 0.123. Cross-validated RMSE 0.157, effectively unbiased.

We reported 12% as the finding, not the failure. Hockey is noisy, and the variables that probably matter (team quality, age, coaching system) weren’t in the data.

Shooting percentage had the strongest positive association, then position (defensemen and centers), assists, and shots per game. Interactive explorer: nhl-explorer.streamlit.app. Interview use: “I know what my model can’t do, and I say so.”
