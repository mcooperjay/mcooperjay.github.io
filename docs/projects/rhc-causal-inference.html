Does a Risky Procedure Actually Increase Mortality?

Causal inference with propensity score matching on Right Heart Catheterization data

Author

Cooper Maughan

Published

March 1, 2026

Overview

Right Heart Catheterization (RHC) is a diagnostic procedure used on critically ill ICU patients. In the raw data, patients who receive RHC die at noticeably higher rates than those who don’t — which raises an uncomfortable question: does the procedure itself increase the risk of death, or are doctors simply choosing to perform it on the patients who were already the sickest?

This is a textbook case of confounding by indication, and it’s a good showcase for why “the numbers say X” is often the wrong takeaway until you’ve asked why the data looks that way.

The Data

The dataset comes from a well-known ICU study and includes treatment status (RHC vs. not), survival outcome, and a wide set of pre-treatment confounders — age, income, insurance type, and physiological severity markers like mean blood pressure, heart rate, and organ dysfunction scores. It’s a genuinely messy, high-dimensional dataset, which is part of what makes it a useful exercise: real clinical data rarely comes clean.

Method

I estimated the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) using propensity score matching:

  • Fit a logistic regression propensity score model using only pre-treatment variables
  • Matched 1:1 nearest-neighbor without replacement, using a caliper of 0.2 on the logit propensity score to exclude implausible matches
  • Checked covariate balance before and after matching with a Love plot and standardized mean differences
  • Estimated the ATT with a weighted logistic regression on the matched sample, incorporating matching weights
m_out <- matchit(
  formula = ps_formula,
  data = RHC_use,
  method   = "nearest",
  distance = "glm",
  estimand = "ATT",
  ratio    = 1,
  caliper  = 0.2,
  replace  = FALSE
)

fit_att <- glm(survival ~ RHC, data = match.data(m_out),
                family = binomial, weights = weights)

I chose a logistic propensity score for interpretability, a tight-ish caliper to prioritize match quality over sample size, and no replacement to avoid a small number of controls dominating the estimate — a defensible but not the only reasonable set of choices, which is part of the point of the exercise.

Results

Before matching, several key severity measures (APACHE score, mean arterial pressure, organ dysfunction markers) showed large imbalances between treated and control patients. After matching, nearly all covariates fell within a standardized mean difference of ±0.1.

The matched-sample estimate: RHC is associated with an approximately 5.1 percentage point decrease in probability of death (95% CI: −8.1 to −2.1 points, p = 0.001).

That’s the opposite direction of the naive, unadjusted comparison — which is the whole story here. Once you account for the fact that RHC was preferentially given to sicker patients, the picture flips substantially. The matching procedure retained about 86% of treated patients (1,870 of 2,184); the remaining 14% were too clinically extreme to find a comparable control, so the estimate applies to the broader population of treated patients but not to that highest-risk subgroup.

What This Demonstrates

  • Recognizing and correcting for confounding by indication, not just fitting a model on raw data
  • Making and defending non-arbitrary modeling choices (caliper, matching ratio, replacement) and understanding their trade-offs
  • Being explicit about the limits of a causal estimate — who it generalizes to and who it doesn’t
  • Comfort with a messy, real-world clinical dataset, not a cleaned-up textbook example
